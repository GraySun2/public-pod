# Launch marketing for a small Steam game: visibility, Next Fest, trailers, creators and press

Steam won't sell visibility, so launch success is built in the months before release: a Coming Soon page early, one Next Fest (a game only gets one), a trailer that shows gameplay in its first seconds, and a steady run of YouTube creators rather than one big launch-day spike. Valve emails wishlisters at release and at discounts of 20% or more, and a crowded release week can bump a game off New & Trending quickly. Creator-driven wishlists convert best in one case study (about 25–30%, against about 14% organic), but results per creator vary widely, and YouTube drives wishlists where Twitch often doesn't. This builds on the Library's [demand validation](../2026-10-22-steam-demand-validation/README.md) study (store page, Next Fest, signups) and adds what it left out: trailer, creators, keys, press and showcases.


**How sure.** "Checked" means read on Valve's page this session. "Secondary" means a search summary of a blog, guide or vendor page. Sources are in [SOURCES.md](SOURCES.md).

## 1. What Steam does for a launch (S1, checked)

- A new release joins a pool of just-released titles, favouring those with the fewest views so far.
- Wishlisters "may receive an e-mail" at release; also when a game leaves Early Access or is discounted 20% or more.
- New & Trending: "Your title may be bumped off this list quickly if many other popular products are releasing at the same time."
- Visibility can't be bought ("Nope"). Store-page traffic and conversion rate are not visibility factors, though sales help indirectly.
- Valve's advice: make sure players know the game is coming, tag accurately, localise (language affects who sees the game), and use a Coming Soon page early: "Some customers may need to see your game dozens of times before it catches their attention."

## 2. Next Fest rules (S2, checked)

- "Only unreleased games are eligible"; "titles may only participate in ONE Next Fest".
- A public demo must be live by the time the fest starts. Early Access or a free launch afterwards is "fine" as long as the game is not yet released.
- Deadlines before the fest: demo build review 5 weeks before (if the demo goes live before Press Preview) or 3 weeks; Press Preview 11 days before; Next Fest trailer 4 days before.
- The page doesn't mention other stores. Whether a paid itch early access counts as "released" stays unconfirmed; the itch plan's free demo is safe either way.

## 3. Trailer (S3, S4, secondary)

- Open with gameplay within the first seconds. Viewers skip logos and slow intros, and many scrub the progress bar looking for raw gameplay. Steam autoplays the first trailer on the store page.
- Length: Derek Lieu suggests 90–180 seconds so scrubbers find variety; another guide says under 90. A small game with little variety should go short.
- Story and cinematics go later, if at all.

## 4. Creators (S5, S6, secondary; How To Market A Game case studies)

- **Conversion.** In the ZERO Sievert case, streamer-driven wishlists converted to sales at about 25–30%, festival wishlists about 15%, organic about 14%.
- **Variance.** One 2.67-million-subscriber channel drove only about 7,000 wishlists for one game; a smaller creator drove 2,700 in days for another.
- **YouTube over Twitch.** One developer saw 25,000 Twitch viewers and no wishlists, against 2,000–3,000 from one YouTube video; Twitch viewers had no store link.
- **Start early, keep going.** Coverage that arrives over weeks builds momentum; creators can't be timed to one day. The author's theory: creators lead to wishlists more than to launch sales.

## 5. Keys and press (S7, S8, secondary)

- Key platforms (Keymailer, Lurkit, Woovit) verify that a requester owns the channel, which stops fake-curator key scams. They add to a targeted email list; they don't replace it. Complaints: dead accounts requesting keys, thresholds that shut out small creators; managed Keymailer bundles reported from $999.
- A press kit page with a key-request form (for example presskit.gg) lets press request keys directly. Pitch on a news moment (demo launch, Next Fest), with a concrete hook, not just "our game exists".

## 6. Showcases and festivals (S9, S10, secondary)

- Curated online showcases now pair with Next Fest. Example: Indie Fan Fest (October 2025) took unreleased games with a new demo for that Next Fest, never shown at an earlier Next Fest; $600 fee if selected; deadline about 10 weeks before.
- Steam runs genre fests through the year; third-party regional festivals exist too. Pick ones whose tags match the game.

## 7. A launch timeline for one game

| When | What |
| --- | --- |
| Concept gate | Title check (see [business and legal basics](../2026-10-23-studio-business-and-legal-basics/README.md)); capsule and tag test per the demand ladder |
| Coming Soon page | Page live with a gameplay-first trailer; start an email or Discord list; first YouTube creator emails |
| Demo live (itch or Steam) | Press-kit page; creator outreach in waves; a devlog per build |
| 10–12 weeks before Next Fest | Apply to one or two curated showcases that pair with it |
| 5 weeks before Next Fest | Demo build in for review |
| Next Fest week | Post a devlog; reply to every creator and player comment |
| Launch | Avoid weeks with a big release in the same tags; a launch discount triggers the 20% wishlist email |

## Recommendations

1. Make one 60–90 second gameplay-first trailer in house before each Coming Soon page.
2. Build a list of 30–50 YouTube creators who cover the game's tags; contact them in waves from the demo onward.
3. Use free key routes (email, a press-kit key form) first; pay for a key platform only after the demo shows interest.
4. Save Next Fest for the strongest demo; each game gets one.

## Unknowns

- Whether a paid itch early access counts as "released" for Next Fest.
- Current creator conversion rates; the case studies run 2020–2025.
